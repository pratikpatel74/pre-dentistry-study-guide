Local Anaesthesia & Pharmacology

Local anaesthetics block sodium channels in nerves. Almost every dental procedure you learn depends on giving them safely, so understand both the chemistry and the cautions.

How local anaesthetics work

  1. The drug is a weak base. Its uncharged form crosses the lipid nerve membrane.
  2. Inside, it picks up H⁺ and the charged form blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels.
  3. No action potential, so no pain signal.

  • Vasoconstrictors (adrenaline, felypressin) make the anaesthetic last longer and reduce bleeding.
  • Aspirate before injecting to avoid a blood vessel.
  • Maximum doses are per kg of body weight. Learn the official figures on your course.

Other medicines you'll meet

  • Painkillers: ibuprofen plus paracetamol is effective for most dental pain. Take care with NSAIDs in some asthma, stomach ulcers and kidney disease.
  • Antibiotics: only for spreading infection or systemic signs, always with treatment of the cause. Amoxicillin or penicillin V first-line; metronidazole (no alcohol); clarithromycin in penicillin allergy.
  • Anticoagulants: warfarin (check INR) and newer DOACs raise bleeding risk after extractions.
  • Bisphosphonates and similar bone drugs: risk of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) after extractions.

Always take an up-to-date medical history before treatment.
